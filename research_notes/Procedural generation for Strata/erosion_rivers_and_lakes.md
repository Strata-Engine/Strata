# Erosion simulation and hydrology (rivers, lakes, drainage) for chunk-streamed voxel terrain

Scope: techniques that make terrain look water-shaped, and which of them can run inside a pure per-chunk function versus needing a global (per-seed) pass. Engine context assumed throughout: a real-time fluid simulation already exists, lakes are currently placed by a deterministic per-map-square rule, and the gap is water-carved terrain plus rivers.

A recurring vocabulary note: "global" below means "needs the whole (or a large bounded) heightmap resident and iterated", "coarse-global" means "a low-resolution map computed once per seed and sampled per chunk", and "chunk-local" means "computable from the chunk's own coordinates and seed, plus a bounded halo".

---

## Key question 1: Hydraulic erosion methods — what each produces, cost, parameters, global vs local

### Takeaway
Particle/droplet erosion is cheap (tens of seconds for 10^5 droplets on 1024² CPU), produces gullies and sediment fans but not coherent river networks, and is global only in the weak sense that droplets must be able to wander across chunk borders; pipe-model (virtual pipes) erosion is a fully parallel per-cell stencil that runs at interactive rates on GPU (2048×1024 at ≥20 fps in 2008) and was explicitly tiled; stream-power/fluvial erosion (Cordonnier 2016, used by Veloren) is the only one of these that produces globally consistent dendritic drainage with downhill-flowing rivers and lakes, but it requires a whole-domain drainage tree and is therefore a per-seed precomputation.

### Cited Findings

**Particle / droplet methods (Beyer 2015; Lague; McDonald)**

- Beyer's droplet model: new direction = old direction × p_inertia − gradient × (1 − p_inertia) (eq. 5.1); carry capacity c = max(−h_diff, p_minSlope) × vel × water × p_capacity (eq. 5.4); if sediment > c, deposit (sediment − c) × p_deposition (eq. 5.5), else erode min((c − sediment) × p_erosion, −h_diff) (eq. 5.6); water_new = water_old × (1 − p_evaporation) (eq. 5.8); droplets live at most p_maxPath steps; erosion is spread over a brush of radius p_radius with weights max(0, p_radius − |P_i − pos|) normalised over the brush. — [Beyer, "Implementation of a method for hydraulic erosion", bachelor thesis (PDF)](https://ardordeosis.github.io/implementation-of-a-method-for-hydraulic-erosion/thesis-beyer.pdf)
- Beyer's figure experiments: 512×512 maps with 300,000 drops, 256×256 maps with 75,000 drops; p_inertia tested at 0.025 / 0.1 / 0.4; p_capacity 2 / 8 / 32; p_deposition 0.01 / 0.1 / 1; p_erosion 0.01 / 0.1 / 0.9; p_evaporation 0.0125 / 0.05 / 0.1; p_radius 2 / 4 / 6 ("if p_radius is 1, very thin ravines occur"); p_minSlope 0.05 / 0.01 / 0.0001. — [Beyer thesis](https://ardordeosis.github.io/implementation-of-a-method-for-hydraulic-erosion/thesis-beyer.pdf)
- Beyer's timing table (Table 6.1; params p_inertia=0.3, p_capacity=8, p_deposition=0.2, p_erosion=0.7, p_evaporation=0.02, p_minSlope=0.01, p_gravity=10, p_radius=4, p_maxPath=64; average of 10 runs): 100,000 drops take 32.0 s on 256², 35.9 s on 512², 40.7 s on 1024², 43.8 s on 2048²; 10,000 drops take 2.3–2.7 s regardless of map size. Time is nearly independent of map size (per-drop cost dominates). Table 6.2: p_radius scales cost sharply — 10,000 drops on 256² take 0.48 s (radius 1), 0.83 s (2), 2.27 s (4), 5.51 s (6), 9.69 s (8). — [Beyer thesis](https://ardordeosis.github.io/implementation-of-a-method-for-hydraulic-erosion/thesis-beyer.pdf)
- Beyer notes that early drops are slower than later ones because ravines, once formed, give later drops a fast path off the map or into a pit; on 100,000 drops roughly 16,500 fell off a 256² map, 12,000 off 512², 5,000 off 1024². — [Beyer thesis](https://ardordeosis.github.io/implementation-of-a-method-for-hydraulic-erosion/thesis-beyer.pdf)
- Sebastian Lague's Unity implementation (a port of Beyer) defaults: erosionRadius 3 (range 2–8), inertia 0.05, sedimentCapacityFactor 4, minSedimentCapacity 0.01, erodeSpeed 0.3, depositSpeed 0.3, evaporateSpeed 0.01, gravity 4, maxDropletLifetime 30, initialWaterVolume 1, initialSpeed 1. Loop: bilinear height+gradient, dir = dir×inertia − gradient×(1−inertia), move 1 unit, capacity = max(−Δh × speed × water × capacityFactor, minCapacity), deposit if sediment > capacity or Δh > 0 (uphill: deposit min(Δh, sediment) to fill), else erode min((capacity − sediment) × erodeSpeed, −Δh) over the brush; speed = sqrt(speed² + Δh × gravity); water ×= (1 − evaporateSpeed). — [Erosion.cs, SebLague/Hydraulic-Erosion](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/SebLague/Hydraulic-Erosion/master/Assets/Scripts/Erosion.cs)
- Lague's README shows the before/after "after 70k iterations of erosion" (one droplet per iteration). — [SebLague/Hydraulic-Erosion](https://github.com/SebLague/Hydraulic-Erosion)
- The erodr C reimplementation of Beyer uses defaults: 70,000 particles, TTL 32, inertia 0.3, capacity 8, gravity 1, evaporation 0.02, erosion 0.7, deposition 0.3, radius 2, min slope 0.0001; it ships a 1024×1024 example and an OpenMP build. — [henrikglass/erodr](https://github.com/henrikglass/erodr)
- Nick McDonald's "Simple Particle-Based Hydraulic Erosion": a particle with position, speed, volume (init 1.0), sediment (init 0.0); per step speed += dt × normal.xz / (volume × density), pos += dt × speed, speed ×= (1 − dt × friction); equilibrium concentration c_eq = volume × |speed| × (h_old − h_new) clamped ≥ 0; sediment += dt × depositionRate × (c_eq − sediment); heightmap[old] −= dt × volume × depositionRate × (c_eq − sediment); volume ×= (1 − dt × evapRate). About 200,000 particles "gives good erosion"; 10–100 µs per particle; 10–20 s total on CPU with defaults. It "does not simulate actual fluid flow or rivers". — [McDonald, Simple Particle-Based Hydraulic Erosion](https://nickmcd.me/2020/04/10/simple-particle-based-hydraulic-erosion/)
- McDonald's follow-up "Procedural Hydrology" adds a stream map (time-averaged particle tracks, stream = 0.99×stream + 0.01×track per batch) and a pool map (water depth per cell), on a 256×256 Perlin heightmap; particles flood-fill a pool when they slow down (stream > 0.5 and |accel| < 0.01) or enter an existing pool; the flood tests a plane at height+pool, 8-neighbour recursive fill, finds the lowest drain cell, and if a drain exists moves the particle there and drains the pool slowly (factor 0.001) with sediment ×0.1; at most 5 pool-spill cycles per particle; volumeFactor 100; per descend+flood step on the order of 1 µs; videos "recorded in real time on 256×256 grids". Author-listed limitations: no flow inside pools (water "teleports" to the outlet), map boundary treated as walls (no sea level), pool level jitters near the drain level, flood-fill scales with pool area and is the bottleneck. — [McDonald, Procedural Hydrology](https://nickmcd.me/2020/04/15/procedural-hydrology/)

**Grid / virtual-pipe methods (Mei et al. 2007; Stava et al. 2008; Jákó & Tóth 2011)**

- Mei, Decaudin, Hu 2007 ("Fast Hydraulic Erosion Simulation and Visualization on GPU", Pacific Graphics 2007, pp. 47–56) stores terrain height, water height, and suspended sediment per cell of a 2D grid, with four virtual pipes per cell to its neighbours; the virtual-pipe idea comes from O'Brien & Hodgins 1995 and "simplifies previous shallow water models" by assuming vertical isotropy; textures hold the data so shaders update it in parallel. — [search summary citing the paper and the HAL preprint](https://hgpu.org/?p=2256) (I could not fetch the full PDF: core.ac.uk returned 403 and HAL was blocked by bot protection, so the paper's own parameter values and fps are a gap below.)
- Jákó & Tóth 2011 ("Fast Hydraulic and Thermal Erosion on the GPU", CESCG 2011), which extends Mei et al., publishes a full parameter table with ranges and typical values: Δt 0.02 [0; 0.05]; rain rate K_r 0.012 [0; 0.05]; evaporation K_e 0.015 [0; 0.05]; pipe cross-section A 20 [0.1; 60]; gravity g 9.81; sediment capacity K_c 1 [0.1; 3]; soil suspension K_s 0.5 [0.1; 2]; deposition K_d 1 [0.1; 3]; sediment softening K_h 5 [0; 10]; max erosion depth K_dmax 10 [0; 40]; talus-angle tangent coefficient K_a 0.8 [0; 1]; talus-angle tangent bias K_i 0.1 [0; 1]; thermal erosion rate K_t 0.15 [0; 3]. Pipe flux: f += Δt × A × g × Δh / l; sediment capacity C = K_c × sin(tilt) × |v|; erode b −= Δt R K_s (C − s) if C > s else deposit b += Δt K_d (s − C); sediment advected by the velocity field; water evaporates d ×= (1 − K_e Δt). — [Jákó & Tóth 2011 (PDF)](https://www.old.cescg.org/CESCG-2011/papers/TUBudapest-Jako-Balazs.pdf)
- Jákó & Tóth performance (ms per iteration, Table 1): 128² CPU 82.8 / 25.7 vs GPU 2.57 / 0.295; 256² CPU 325 / 102 vs GPU 9.92 / 1.01; 512² CPU 1289 / 413 vs GPU 39.2 / 3.84; 1024² CPU 5166 / 1647 vs GPU 160 / 15.5 (two hardware configurations each); GPU/CPU ratio ~32× and ~107×. — [Jákó & Tóth 2011](https://www.old.cescg.org/CESCG-2011/papers/TUBudapest-Jako-Balazs.pdf)
- Jákó & Tóth thermal erosion uses eight virtual pipes (4-neighbours plus diagonals) and moves material from a cell to each neighbour whose slope exceeds the talus angle, the per-neighbour share proportional to its height difference; they say pure hydraulic erosion "carves deep grooves into the surface that rarely occurs in nature", which is why thermal erosion was added. — [Jákó & Tóth 2011](https://www.old.cescg.org/CESCG-2011/papers/TUBudapest-Jako-Balazs.pdf)
- Šťava, Beneš, Brisbin, Křivánek 2008 ("Interactive Terrain Modeling Using Hydraulic Erosion", SCA 2008): GPU pipe-model erosion of layered terrain runs "at least at 20 fps on off-the-shelf computers for scenes with grid resolution of 2048 × 1024 and four layers of material" (NVIDIA GeForce 8800 GTX); couples dissolution erosion (slow water) with force-based erosion (fast water) and slippage of undercut banks; 32-bit float textures are required ("16-bit precision may results in serious issues with water flow"). — [Šťava et al. 2008 (PDF)](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Stava08SCA.pdf)
- Šťava et al. tiling: "To address the limited GPU memory, we divide the terrain into tiles" that "are calculated independently and synchronized at borders" (Figure 8); boundary values are copied in before each simulation step and "tile dimensions and computational domain are extended to handle these copied values", which also allows eroding only a selected sub-region such as one river. — [Šťava et al. 2008](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Stava08SCA.pdf)
- Šťava's thermal (slippage) rule for layered material: amount moved = Δt (Δh − l tan α) when Δh > l tan α, with α the material's talus angle; layers are processed bottom-to-top for slippage and top-to-bottom for hydraulic erosion. — [Šťava et al. 2008](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Stava08SCA.pdf)

**Stream power / fluvial erosion (Cordonnier 2016; Schott 2023; Veloren)**

- Cordonnier et al. 2016 (Eurographics/CGF 35(2)) takes a user-painted uplift map, builds a stream graph over the whole domain and solves the stream power equation dh/dt = u − k A^m |∇h|^n; they use n = 1 and m = 0.5 ("as in most geomorphological studies", m/n ≈ 0.5 constrains river profiles). — [Cordonnier et al. 2016 (PDF)](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Cordonier16CGF.pdf)
- Cordonnier's graph is not a regular grid: nodes are Poisson-disk samples with a constrained Delaunay triangulation, because "the randomized triangular graph generates more plausible results" (edges better represent possible stream directions) and quasi-uniform edge length sets the feature level of detail; border nodes are fixed at h = 0 and act as river mouths / outflows. — [Cordonnier et al. 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Cordonier16CGF.pdf)
- Implicit solver (Braun & Willett 2013 style): with n = 1, h_i(t+δt) = (h_i(t) + δt u_i + δt k A_i^m h_j(t+δt)/|p_i − p_j|) / (1 + δt k A_i^m/|p_i − p_j|), evaluated from the downstream receiver j upward, which "requires that h_j(t+δt) should be computed before h_i(t+δt)" — the stack order of the drainage tree. — [Cordonnier et al. 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Cordonier16CGF.pdf)
- Cordonnier experiment parameters: domain 50 × 50 km²; max uplift U = 5.0×10⁻⁴ m/yr ("the average uplift among earth mountains"); erosion rate k = 5.61×10⁻⁷ yr⁻¹ chosen so mountains culminate at about 2,000 m; time step δt = 2.5×10⁵ yr "to ensure a fast convergence while avoiding the appearance of high unnatural cliffs"; thermal erosion slope limits 6° and 54°. — [Cordonnier et al. 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Cordonier16CGF.pdf)
- Cordonnier timing (Table 1, Intel Core i7 3 GHz, 16 GB, CPU): 10,000 samples 0.031 s/step, converged in 6.4 s; 40,000 samples 0.177 s/step, 35.4 s; 90,000 samples 0.401 s/step, 78.0 s; 160,000 samples 1.273 s/step, 252 s. "Convergence is obtained after around 200 time steps"; mountains fully shaped after ~50 iterations and geometry stops evolving after 100–300; because the solver is implicit, iteration count "does not depend on the resolution of the terrain". — [Cordonnier et al. 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Cordonier16CGF.pdf)
- A 2022 reimplementation benchmark on a regular grid reports Cordonnier's algorithm at 100 iterations taking 7.3 s (256²), 29.4 s (512²), 124.8 s (1024²), versus their own variant 7.5 / 31.3 / 115.6 s. — [Lim, Tan, Bhojan 2022, "Visually Improved Erosion Algorithm for the Procedural Generation of Tile-Based Terrain" (arXiv 2210.14496)](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2210.14496)
- Schott, Paris, Fournier, Guérin, Galin 2023 ("Large-scale terrain authoring through interactive erosion simulation", ACM TOG 42(5), SIGGRAPH 2023) authors in the uplift domain and computes relief by stream power erosion using "a fast but accurate approximation of drainage area and flow routing" to make erosion interactive and incremental; it includes "hydrologically consistent blending between terrain patches" and inverse modelling (uplift from an input DEM). Code: github.com/H-Schott/StreamPowerErosion. — [search summary / SIGGRAPH history entry](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=132308); [repo](https://github.com/H-Schott/StreamPowerErosion). I could not fetch the paper PDF (HAL bot protection), so its grid sizes, ms/iteration, and GPU/CPU details are a gap.
- Veloren (open-source voxel RPG) uses this family: its `erosion.rs` doc comment gives dh/dt = uplift − k·A^m·slope^n with m = 0.4, n = 1, k = 2.244 × uplift_max / desired_max_height; a debris-flow term with area exponent q = 0.2 and slope exponent q_ = 1.5 (comment: setting q_ = 1.0 linearises the problem "for a significant speedup"); dt = max_uplift / 1e-3; sediment layer thickness 1e-4 × dt above which k_fs is multiplied by 4; Fastscape-style stack-order Gauss–Seidel iteration with up to 99 sweeps and RMSE tolerance max(1e-4(maxh+1), 1e-3(max_uplift+1)); Newton–Raphson local solve when n ≠ 1; talus limits from a rock-strength noise field between 15° and 60° (mid-slope 30°); thermal erosion collapses material to the lowest neighbour when slope exceeds talus, otherwise a nonlinear hillslope-transport term capped at min_length²/dt; final hillslope diffusion via an ADI scheme ported from fastscape's Diffusion.f90; soil production ε₀ exp(−αH); kdsed = 1.5e-2/4 scaled by k_d_scale; drainage accumulated multi-receiver from base area 1.0 per chunk. — [veloren_world/sim/erosion.rs (docs.veloren.net source view)](https://docs.veloren.net/src/veloren_world/sim/erosion.rs.html)
- Veloren river geometry from drainage: Q = drainage × chunk_area × rainfall_chunk_rate; width = 5 × (w2d (w2d+2)^(2/3))^(3/8) × Q^(3/8) × S^(−3/16) × roughness^(3/8) (w2d = river_width_to_depth); depth = (Q/(sqrt(S)) × roughness / width)^(3/5); a chunk is a river if width ≥ 0.5 and depth ≥ river_min_height; width capped at RECT_SIZE.x × river_max_width; velocity = (1/roughness) × depth^(2/3) × sqrt(S) (Manning). — [erosion.rs](https://docs.veloren.net/src/veloren_world/sim/erosion.rs.html)
- Veloren devblog 43: "noise cannot produce river networks that fit the terrain"; their method is a hybrid — explicit drainage network per time step (single receiver per node), implicit solve of the shear-stress ODE along it, "which allows much larger time steps"; combined with tectonic uplift, thermal erosion ("breaks down rock steeper than its angle of repose"), and a fully implicit hillslope diffusion step that "smooths hills, gentles beach slopes, fills small depressions, and creates oxbow lakes by blocking rivers with sediment". Rivers and lakes "are guaranteed to flow downhill and connect globally". Noise still sets uplift rate, initial continent shape, rock strength, and diffusion rate. — [This Week in Veloren 43](https://veloren.net/devblog-43)
- Veloren devblog 36: naive erosion stops rivers at depressions and either needs an always-downhill map or depression filling each iteration, which "removes lakes and is slow" (~O(n^1.5) for best-known algorithms); their fix treats each lake as one abstract node, finds lowest passes between neighbouring lakes, and traces from the boundary, in ~O(n + m log m); the implicit method "allows you to choose whatever delta t and erosion level you want without destroying convergence"; each map chunk is one node of the erosion graph. — [devblog 36 (mirror)](https://planet.freegamedev.net/?p=149525)
- A mirrored Veloren post states that after the rivers/lakes/erosion merge, worldgen "simulates 12.5 million years of erosion", so singleplayer startup can take "a minute or two". — [planet.freegamedev.net mirror](https://planet.freegamedev.net/?p=149535) (page fetch failed; this is from the search snippet only.)
- Veloren's book describes a three-stage pipeline: a **geological stage** (noise for altitude, rock strength, humidity, temperature → erosion algorithm → altitude map saved as a `.bin` asset), a **filling stage** at server start that "uses the geological data to place lakes and rivers" and decides "whether depressions in the terrain should fill with liquid", and a **reshaping stage** adding local cliffs and caves. — [Veloren book, Worldgen (WIP)](https://book.veloren.net/internals/worldgen/worldgen.html)

### Inferences
- Droplet erosion is **halo-local**: a droplet's lifetime is bounded (30–64 steps, 1 cell/step), so a chunk could be eroded deterministically using a halo of ~maxPath cells of neighbouring terrain and a per-chunk droplet seed. But droplets entering from outside the halo are not accounted for, so cross-chunk gullies will not line up unless droplets are spawned by a region-level (not chunk-level) rule and both chunks replay the same droplets. Given Beyer's timings (~0.3–0.4 ms per drop at radius 4), 1,000 drops per 32×32 chunk costs ~0.3 s — too much for a streaming budget unless the radius is 1–2 and the count is in the low hundreds.
- Pipe-model erosion is a per-cell stencil; Šťava's tiling with border synchronisation is exactly the pattern a chunk engine could use on a **coarse** grid, but it is only consistent if all tiles run in lockstep, i.e. it is really a global pass that happens to be tiled for memory, not an independent per-chunk pass.
- Stream power erosion is irreducibly global (receiver tree, drainage area) but is cheap enough at coarse resolution: Cordonnier's 160k samples converge in ~4 minutes single-threaded CPU; 40k samples in 35 s. A 512×512 coarse map at 1 sample per 16 blocks covers an 8 km × 8 km world and would take on the order of minutes once per seed on CPU, or seconds with Veloren-style rayon parallelism.
- Veloren is the closest existing precedent: it runs Cordonnier-style erosion at one node per chunk, ships the result as an asset, then derives rivers (width/depth from drainage) and lakes per chunk at sample time.

## Key question 2: Rivers without full simulation

### Takeaway
There is a hard dichotomy: noise-band rivers (Minecraft-style) are chunk-local and cheap but sit at a constant level and do not flow downhill; downhill-flowing rivers require a drainage computation over a bounded region (a whole map, a Voronoi "plate" of ~6,700 world units, or Veloren's per-chunk graph) and so are at best region-local, never purely per-block.

### Cited Findings

**Minecraft (1.18+)**

- Rivers in modern Minecraft are a biome placed by noise, "generated in a Context Free manner, using various noise techniques" (Red Blob speculates "edge detection + carving?" but marks it unverified). — [search summary of community/wiki posts](https://minecraft.fandom.com/wiki/River); [Red Blob rivers draft](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/rivers/)
- Peaks-and-valleys (PV) = 1 − |3|weirdness| − 2|; PV bands: Valleys (−1.0 to −0.85), Low (−0.85 to −0.2), Mid (−0.2 to 0.2), High (0.2 to 0.7), Peaks (0.7 to 1.0); "the higher the PV value, the higher the terrain". Rivers form where continentalness is low or erosion is high and PV is "Valleys" because the terrain there is "low enough to generate rivers"; River / Frozen River occupy the Valleys row across erosion levels in the biome table. — [Minecraft Wiki, World generation](https://minecraft.wiki/w/World_generation)
- Terrain height offset comes from a spline of continentalness, erosion and PV; a separate "factor" spline sets vertical squashing; continentalness bands: ocean roughly −1.05 to −0.19, mid-inland from ~0.03, far-inland from ~0.3; erosion has seven levels with thresholds such as −0.375 and 0.05. — [Minecraft Wiki, World generation](https://minecraft.wiki/w/World_generation)
- "River basins are filled with still water up to sea level"; Overworld sea_level = 63; a 1.18 change made mountain rivers "either carve a fjord through it, or they raise the terrain to form a valley" instead of a dry gorge; low erosion inland can make rivers end abruptly and high erosion inland can make them dry; rivers override canyon carvers near the surface. — [Minecraft Wiki, River](https://minecraft.wiki/w/River); [Minecraft Wiki, Noise settings](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Noise_settings)
- The weirdness ("ridges") noise is "only used for biome placement" in the noise router; terrain shape is driven by final_density. — [Minecraft Wiki, Noise router](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Noise_router)
- Because every column's value is computed independently from noise (the whole point of the density-function design), the river band is simply where the offset spline pushes terrain to or below sea level along the weirdness zero-crossing band; the water is the global "default fluid up to sea level" rule, not routed flow. (This is my reading of the wiki's "basins filled with still water up to sea level" plus the noise-router description; the wiki never states "rivers do not flow downhill" in those words.) — [Minecraft Wiki, Aquifer: global fluid rule](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Aquifer)

**"River noise" (|noise| < threshold)**

- Ridged noise is defined as r(p) = 1 − |n(p)| to create sharp crests; its zero-crossing curves are therefore a natural set of continuous lines on an infinite domain (Galin et al. review via search). — [Galin et al., A Review of Digital Terrain Modeling (search summary)](https://wenku.csdn.net/doc/3h4apwpp1n)
- Red Blob's noise-terrain page: "Local calculation means every location is independent of every other location", which is why pure noise cannot route water downhill, and there are no "global features like a river flowing from the top of the highest peak down to the ocean"; the upside is parallelism and infinite terrain by offsetting noise coordinates. — [Red Blob Games, Making maps with noise functions](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/terrain-from-noise/)
- A Boundless forum thread describes a threshold-carving river implementation in a voxel game and alignment problems between river noise and carved caves (anecdotal). — [search summary](https://forum.playboundless.com/t/noise-ghosting/5249)
- I found no primary source stating runtime figures for |noise|-threshold rivers; cost is one extra 2D noise evaluation per column (inference).

**Red Blob Games polygonal maps and the rivers draft**

- Red Blob's rivers draft: each land node flows to its lowest neighbour if that neighbour is lower; each node has at most one outflow, so "rivers can merge but never split"; mapgen2 picks random source points preferring high elevation, mapgen4 computes rainfall at every point and draws only the larger rivers; Hack's law (river length ∝ area^0.6) noted but not implemented; regular grids "produce visible artifacts and overly regular rivers". — [Red Blob Games, Rivers (draft)](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/rivers/)
- On infinite maps: "I think we have to loosen our requirements to get rivers on an infinite map" because water in one region depends on rainfall far upstream; proposed loosenings (none implemented): limit river length, allow rivers to not always flow downhill, or generate rivers first and adjust elevation to match ("I didn't get this working"). — [Red Blob Games, Rivers (draft)](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/rivers/)
- The original polygonal map page could not be fetched in this session (ECONNRESET twice); its river method is summarised in the rivers draft above (downslope pointers on Voronoi corners, random sources, merging).

**Plate-local rivers (arXiv 2610.00301)**

- Davis, "Plate-Local River Generation: Deterministic, Terrain-Aware, Downhill-Flowing Rivers for Infinite Procedural Worlds" (arXiv 2610.00301, Sep 2026): plate seeds on an integer lattice with jitter ≤ 1/3 cell; plate-space scale s_p = 1.5×10⁻⁴ so one plate cell ≈ 6,667 world units; each plate samples a 100×100 low-res grid over its bounding rectangle, traces rivers from highland sources to coastlines/local minima/confluences, adjusts stored heights so every segment descends; at full resolution the engine computes exact distance to the cached network, interpolates river height and blends it with terrain on demand. — [arXiv 2610.00301 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.00301v1)
- Border handling: warp amplitude a_w = 0.01 plate cells (max border displacement ≈ 94 world units), border margin ω_border = 100 world units, river blend width 100, lake radius 150; "Rivers therefore never drain into a plate border" — rivers cannot cross plate boundaries, and the design relies on oceans/plateaus to discourage cross-boundary drainage; only 6 of 92,800 blend-band samples (0.0065%) fell on a neighbouring plate. — [arXiv 2610.00301](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.00301v1)
- Performance (Apple M4 Max, medians of 5 cold runs): per-plate preprocessing mean 3.81 ms (range 2.33–10.07 ms) for 10 plates; per-sample terrain 1.13 µs with rivers vs 0.27 µs without (~0.85 µs for river queries); memory 0.169 MiB river cache + 4.10 MiB planning grids (discardable). Limitations: O(|R|) scan per sample (no spatial index), only 44.4% of candidate paths accepted (222/500), max Strahler order 2, single seed tested. — [arXiv 2610.00301](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.00301v1)

**Flow accumulation on a coarse map then upsampling; Genevaux 2013**

- Genevaux et al. 2013 ("Terrain Generation Using Procedural Models Based on Hydrology", ACM TOG 32(4)) grows the river network *first* from river mouths on the coastline inward using Horton–Strahler-indexed grammar rules (candidate selection: lowest elevation z, admissible set within [z, z+ζ], highest priority, then smallest elevation), then partitions the domain into Voronoi cells of river nodes, computes watersheds, and builds terrain as a construction tree of primitives with blending and carving operators; the river slope map and terrain slope map are the two user controls. — [Genevaux et al. 2013 (PDF)](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Genevaux13ToG.pdf)
- Genevaux timings (Table 2, seconds): graph generation 0.1–5.3 s, flows/watershed 0.1–1.1 s, tree construction 1.8–4.9 s, evaluation of a 1024×1024 mesh 0.7–1.2 s; output is vector/analytic, "average of 1 km² ≈ 1.5 kB"; Hoya Island 3,368 km² = 81,853 primitives in 8,180 kB, and "the user can evaluate only a portion of the landscape". — [Genevaux et al. 2013](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Genevaux13ToG.pdf)
- The monoamin/rivergen design (Minecraft mod concept) builds a per-chunk drainage graph (grid nodes with elevation, directed edges downhill), traces each river source to a sink chunk by chunk without going uphill, carves with a parabolic/inverse-bell profile after `buildSurface`, and must pre-generate "tendrils" of chunks beyond the player so rivers can be traced; it notes rivers of useful length "need terrain that slopes consistently over distances larger than one chunk, which local generation doesn't provide", and estimates ~100 ms per chunk to `fillFromNoise`, "roughly 6 to 8 minutes for a 2x2 region of 1024 chunks". — [monoamin/rivergen README](https://github.com/monoamin/rivergen)

**Dwarf Fortress**

- DF worldgen order (wiki): biomes from elevation/temperature/rainfall/drainage, then "erosion and the creation of rivers are simulated", then lakes, "river loops created by the previous step are fixed", then "flow amounts are calculated to determine which rivers are tributaries"; rivers "are created by tracing their paths from the mountains (which get eroded) to its end which is usually an ocean; some form into lakes"; "mountains get eaten and fractured by rivers and erosion"; an erosion-cycle count is an advanced worldgen parameter. — [search summary of dwarffortresswiki 40d:World and Advanced World Generation](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/40d:World) (the wiki returned 429 on direct fetch; the algorithm itself is not documented in detail anywhere I found.)

### Inferences
- For a chunk-streamed engine the realistic options are, in increasing cost and realism: (a) Minecraft-style noise band at sea level (zero global state, no downhill flow, consistent with the existing "water up to level" fluid rule); (b) plate/region-local drainage: pick a region size (Davis uses ~6.7k units with a 100² planning grid and 3–4 ms preprocessing per region), trace rivers inside it, forbid crossing region borders, cache the polyline network, and blend per sample; (c) a per-seed coarse global map with flow accumulation (or full stream-power erosion) and per-chunk sampling as Veloren does.
- Option (b) keeps determinism (region seed) and bounded cost, at the price of rivers that never cross region borders — acceptable if regions are large and borders fall on ridges/oceans by construction.
- Genevaux's "network first, terrain second" is attractive for chunking because the output is a compact vector network (≈1.5 kB/km²) that any chunk can query; the catch is that the network is grown from the coast inward over a bounded domain, so it is per-seed, not per-chunk.

### Gaps
- No primary source gives the exact Minecraft offset-spline control points that put the river band below sea level; the wiki describes the mechanism qualitatively only.
- Dwarf Fortress's actual erosion/river algorithm is undocumented beyond the stage order.
- Veloren's "filling stage" details (how a chunk decides it is river vs lake at sample time, chunk size in blocks, map size in chunks) were not in the pages reached; `erosion.rs` references `TerrainChunkSize::RECT_SIZE` and `MapSizeLg` without values.

## Key question 3: Lakes — depressions, flat water levels, chunk-border consistency, aquifers, non-draining basins

### Takeaway
A lake that will not drain must sit in a closed depression whose rim is above the water level; the standard tool for finding depressions and their spill heights is priority-flood (O(n) integer / O(n log n) float), which is inherently a whole-map pass, while Minecraft's aquifers show the opposite design — water levels assigned per 3D cell from noise with stone barriers forced wherever adjacent cells disagree, which is chunk-local but does not guarantee hydrological consistency.

### Cited Findings

**Depression filling / priority-flood**

- Barnes, Lehman, Mulla 2014 (Computers & Geosciences): Priority-Flood floods inward from the DEM edge using a priority queue; result "has no depressions or digital dams, so every cell is guaranteed to drain"; O(n) for integer data, O(n lg n) for floating-point, "optimal for both"; a variant switching to a plain queue inside a depression reaches O(m lg m), m ≤ n, "the lowest known time complexity for floating-point depression filling"; the improved variant ran "up to 37% faster" than the original, and a parallel version of an older algorithm "needed six processors to match" it on one. Can label watersheds and produce flow directions by incremental elevation (epsilon) or by carving. — [Barnes et al., arXiv 1511.04463](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.04463)
- Reference implementation includes an epsilon variant: "every cell which would have been in a depression is raised to the level of that depression's outlet, plus an additional increment sufficient to direct flow to the periphery". — [r-barnes/Barnes2013-Depressions](https://github.com/r-barnes/Barnes2013-Depressions)
- Barnes 2016: a tiled, parallel Priority-Flood "for trillion cell DEMs on desktops or clusters" that "linearly scales and parallelizes Priority-Flood by subdividing a DEM into tiles" (tiles are processed independently, then a global graph of tile-edge spill heights is solved, then tiles are corrected). — [Barnes 2016, arXiv 1606.06204](https://arxiv.org/html/1606.06204)
- Veloren's `fill_sinks` implements Planchon–Darboux in its "exact" form (epsilon = 0), preserving altitudes; `get_lakes` marks lake roots (downhill = −1 interior, −2 boundary), ranks candidate passes by max(h_from, h_to) in a min-heap so the lowest pass is chosen first, gives the lake bottom a downhill pointer across the chosen pass, and then BFS-assigns every lake node a single receiver preferring neighbours aligned with the outflow direction and at or below the current elevation. — [erosion.rs](https://docs.veloren.net/src/veloren_world/sim/erosion.rs.html)
- Cordonnier's lake handling: lakes are tree roots not on the boundary; all nodes draining to a root share its lake id; a super-graph of lakes is built with "passes" (saddle points) between adjacent lakes, and overflow is routed to river mouths in O(N + M log M) with M = number of lakes, versus O(N√N) for the naive approach; without lake connection the simulation needs 4–5 extra iterations and "a large number of local minima are still adding some discontinuities in the hydrology network". — [Cordonnier et al. 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Cordonier16CGF.pdf)
- McDonald's pool flooding (particle-driven): raise a test plane from height+pool, flood-fill 8-neighbours, record cells below the initial level as drain candidates, keep the lowest; if a drain exists the pool overflows there, otherwise commit the fill if the particle's volume covers Σ volumeFactor × (plane − (height+pool)); plane adjusted by 0.5 × (volume − tVol)/|set|/volumeFactor; up to 10 failures before discarding. Known artefacts: pool level jitters near the drain; map edges act as walls. — [McDonald, Procedural Hydrology](https://nickmcd.me/2020/04/15/procedural-hydrology/)

**Minecraft aquifers (per-region water levels sampled per block)**

- Aquifer cells are 16×12×16 blocks, offset 5 blocks on X/Z and 1 on Y from chunk boundaries; each cell has one centre, randomly offset 0–9 on X/Z and 0–8 on Y from the seed and cell coords; each centre holds a status (fluid level + type, or dry). A block looks at the 12 surrounding cells (2×3×2), finds the four nearest centres by squared distance, and takes the nearest centre's status if it is closer than the second by ≥ 25 (squared distance); otherwise barrier logic applies. Result: a 3D Voronoi-like pattern whose levels "change in steps at cell boundaries rather than smoothly". — [Minecraft Wiki, Aquifer](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Aquifer)
- Global fluid rule (no aquifer): below min(−54, sea level) lava with surface at Y = −54; otherwise the default fluid up to sea level. Oceans and rivers "come from the global fluid rule". — [Minecraft Wiki, Aquifer](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Aquifer)
- Cell status: the preliminary surface level (a density function, slightly below true terrain) is sampled at 13 chunk offsets (own chunk plus 3 west, 1 east, 1 north/south), each raised by 8 blocks; a cell whose bottom (centre Y − 12) is above its own chunk's raised surface uses the global rule (air if high); a cell whose top (centre Y + 12) rises above any sampled surface that is below sea level becomes "sea" (level = sea level); otherwise it is underground and decided by `fluid_level_floodedness` noise (clamped to [−1,1]) with thresholds −0.3/−0.8 (fully/partially flooded) just under a sub-sea surface and 0.8/0.4 for cells 64+ blocks deeper or on land, linearly interpolated between. — [Minecraft Wiki, Aquifer](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Aquifer)
- Partially flooded cells get level = 40 × floor(Y/40) + 20 + spread, spread ∈ {−10, …, +10} in steps of 3 from `fluid_level_spread` sampled once per 16×40×16 region, capped at the lowest sampled surface, so levels cluster near Y = −20, 20, 60, 100…; partially flooded cells can sit above sea level, giving lakes in mountain caves. Lava if level ≤ −10 and |lava noise| > 0.3, sampled once per 64×40×64 region. No aquifers where erosion < −0.225 and depth > 0.9 (Deep Dark). — [Minecraft Wiki, Aquifer](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Aquifer)
- Barriers: where the two or three nearest centres are nearly equidistant (squared-distance difference < 25) and their statuses differ, a pressure term is added to terrain density and the block becomes stone if the sum is positive; the pressure is strongest between the two levels, falls off quickly above the higher level (lids up to ~5 blocks) and slowly below the lower (floors/walls up to ~23 blocks); water next to lava gets a strong fixed pressure; `barrier` noise randomises it in a narrow band; identical statuses give zero pressure so a body has no internal walls. Fluid blocks at status boundaries get a scheduled tick after generation and flow if they touch air (waterfalls). — [Minecraft Wiki, Aquifer](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Aquifer)

**Veloren lake placement**

- Veloren's filling stage "decides whether depressions in the terrain should fill with liquid" using the geological-stage data, i.e. lakes are derived from the eroded map rather than placed independently. — [Veloren book](https://book.veloren.net/internals/worldgen/worldgen.html)
- Veloren's hillslope diffusion step "fills small depressions, and creates oxbow lakes by blocking rivers with sediment". — [devblog 43](https://veloren.net/devblog-43)

### Inferences
- "Lake at a consistent level across chunk borders" is solved identically by Minecraft and by the engine's existing per-map-square rule: the level is a function of a region id, not of the chunk. The unsolved part is "sits in a genuine basin": that requires the terrain function to know the rim height. Two ways to get it chunk-locally: (1) make the lake *define* the terrain — subtract a bowl whose rim is forced above the water level inside the region (the lake radius/blend band pattern of Davis's plates, lake radius 150 units, blend 100), or (2) compute the basin spill height on a coarse map with priority-flood (a per-seed pass) and set the level to spill − ε.
- Minecraft's barrier trick (force stone wherever two adjacent water-level cells disagree) is a cheap chunk-local way to prevent one region's lake from draining into a neighbour's lower level; applied at the surface rather than underground it would read as an artificial dam, so it is better suited to caves/aquifers than to surface lakes.
- A fluid simulation that drains lakes when their rims are carved (as in the target engine) is exactly the failure mode priority-flood prevents in offline tools; using the coarse-map spill height to set the level, and leaving the fluid sim to handle player-made breaches, is the consistent division of labour.

### Gaps
- No source found describing how Veloren decides the *level* of a lake per chunk at sample time (the `get_lakes` pass heights imply level = lowest pass, but I did not see the sampling code).
- No published numbers found for priority-flood runtimes on specific sizes (the arXiv abstract gives only relative speedups).

## Key question 4: Chunk-local strategies — coarse global maps, regions, deterministic seeding, two-pass pipelines

### Takeaway
Every shipped system that has downhill rivers precomputes *something* per seed (Veloren: a full eroded chunk-resolution map; Davis: a per-plate river cache; Genevaux: a vector network), and every system with zero precomputation (Minecraft, |noise| bands) gives up downhill flow; the practical middle ground is a coarse per-seed map at 1 sample per chunk (Veloren's granularity), with region-level caches generated lazily and deterministically from a region seed.

### Cited Findings

- Veloren treats "each map chunk as one point in the erosion graph" and runs the whole erosion offline, saving the altitude map as a `.bin` asset; per-chunk generation then samples that map (filling stage outputs per-chunk data). — [devblog 36 mirror](https://planet.freegamedev.net/?p=149525); [Veloren book](https://book.veloren.net/internals/worldgen/worldgen.html)
- Veloren's river data is per chunk (width, depth, velocity derived from drainage via Manning's equation, with a chunk flagged as river if width ≥ 0.5 and depth ≥ river_min_height), and drainage uses "base area 1.0 per chunk". — [erosion.rs](https://docs.veloren.net/src/veloren_world/sim/erosion.rs.html)
- Davis 2026: regions ("plates") of ~6,667 world units are generated "lazily, one plate at a time" from a lattice seed with ≤ 1/3-cell jitter, with no global preprocessing; each plate's 100×100 planning grid costs ~3–4 ms; the cache is 0.17 MiB per 10 plates; the per-sample cost with rivers is 1.13 µs. — [arXiv 2610.00301](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.00301v1)
- Minecraft samples aquifer status per 16×12×16 cell (centre jittered by seed+cell coords), `fluid_level_spread` per 16×40×16, lava per 64×40×64, and the preliminary surface at 13 chunk offsets — i.e. a hierarchy of deterministic region granularities, all evaluable from (seed, coords). — [Minecraft Wiki, Aquifer](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Aquifer)
- Minecraft's density functions have explicit caching primitives: `cache` (compute once per block position when referenced twice), `cache_2d` (once per horizontal position), `flat_cache` (once per 4×4 column at Y = 0), and `interpolated` with `cell_size_xz` / `cell_size_y` (the wiki prose says cells are size_horizontal×4 by size_vertical×4 blocks; the page is internally inconsistent on naming and these fields were removed/replaced by `upscale` in 26.3–26.4 snapshots). — [Minecraft Wiki, Density function](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Density_function); [Noise settings](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Noise_settings)
- Genevaux's analytic terrain lets "the user … evaluate only a portion of the landscape" from a compact construction tree (a 30 km² portion = 2,068 primitives, 225 kB), with evaluation of 1024×1024 samples in ~1 s. — [Genevaux et al. 2013](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Genevaux13ToG.pdf)
- Šťava 2008 tiles are "calculated independently and synchronized at borders" with copied border values and extended computational domains per tile. — [Šťava et al. 2008](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Stava08SCA.pdf)
- Barnes 2016 shows depression filling itself can be tiled: tiles processed independently, then a global graph of tile-edge spill elevations is solved, then each tile is corrected. — [Barnes 2016, arXiv 1606.06204](https://arxiv.org/html/1606.06204)
- Cordonnier notes that thanks to the implicit solver, iteration count "does not depend on the resolution of the terrain", and suggests accelerating convergence by progressively refining the sampling grid (uniformly). — [Cordonnier et al. 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Cordonier16CGF.pdf)
- Cordonnier also shows fluvial erosion applied *without* uplift to an existing fractal terrain "adds a global hydrological realism", but then "it is necessary to use small time steps and to stop the simulation after only a few iterations" because it converges toward flat. — [Cordonnier et al. 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Cordonier16CGF.pdf)
- rivergen's design must pre-generate chunk "tendrils" beyond the player to trace rivers, discards the drainage graph after carving, and acknowledges that local generation does not provide consistent slope over distances larger than one chunk. — [monoamin/rivergen](https://github.com/monoamin/rivergen)

### Inferences
- Two-pass pipeline that fits the target engine: **pass 1 (per seed, cached)** — build a coarse heightmap at 1 sample per 8–32 blocks from the same base noise the chunk function uses, run a bounded number of implicit stream-power iterations (Cordonnier: ~50 iterations shape mountains; 100–300 converge; at 1024² the reference cost is ~1.2 s/iteration single-thread, so 50 iterations ≈ 1 minute, parallelisable), then priority-flood it to get per-basin spill heights, drainage area, and receiver directions; **pass 2 (per chunk)** — bilinearly sample coarse height/drainage, add high-frequency noise whose amplitude is damped near river cells, carve a channel whose width/depth follow Veloren's Q^(3/8) rule, and set lake level = basin spill height − ε.
- The coarse map must be finite (or lazily generated in large regions with Davis-style "rivers never cross a region border" constraints). An infinite world cannot have a single consistent global drainage without either a finite map or that compromise.
- Chunk-border consistency is automatic if every chunk samples the same immutable coarse map; the only per-chunk randomness that can safely differ is high-frequency detail that does not move the drainage line by more than the channel half-width.

### Gaps
- No source gave a measured end-to-end time for "coarse erosion + per-chunk upsampling" in a voxel engine other than Veloren's "a minute or two" startup anecdote and rivergen's ~100 ms/chunk estimate.
- Veloren's map dimensions in chunks and chunk size in blocks were not in the fetched pages.

## Key question 5: GPU / compute-shader erosion — worth it for a Vulkan engine?

### Takeaway
Pipe-model (grid) erosion is embarrassingly parallel and gains 30–100× on GPU (Jákó: 1024² at 15–160 ms/iteration on GPU vs 1.6–5.2 s on CPU; Šťava: 2048×1024 ≥ 20 fps), whereas stream-power erosion is a serial tree traversal that the reference implementations run on CPU and converge in seconds-to-minutes at coarse resolution — so a GPU is worth it only if the engine wants a *fine-resolution* pipe-model pass (e.g. at chunk load) rather than a coarse per-seed drainage map.

### Cited Findings

- Jákó & Tóth 2011: GPU/CPU speed ratios of ~32× (config 1) and ~87–107× (config 2) across 128²–1024²; 1024² iteration = 160 ms (GPU1) / 15.5 ms (GPU2); implemented as fragment shaders over ping-pong framebuffer objects with one full-screen quad per iteration. — [Jákó & Tóth 2011](https://www.old.cescg.org/CESCG-2011/papers/TUBudapest-Jako-Balazs.pdf)
- Šťava 2008: ≥ 20 fps at 2048×1024 with four material layers on a GeForce 8800 GTX; all data stays on the GPU "without any data transfer from GPU to CPU during the simulation"; needs 32-bit float textures; Shader Model 4. — [Šťava et al. 2008](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Stava08SCA.pdf)
- Cordonnier 2016 timings are CPU (Core i7 3 GHz): 160k nodes at 1.27 s/step. The implicit scheme requires downstream nodes to be solved before upstream ones. — [Cordonnier et al. 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Cordonier16CGF.pdf)
- Veloren runs its erosion on CPU with rayon thread pools and an optional SIMD feature, and the source notes multi-receiver drainage is costlier than single-receiver and that linearising (q_ = 1) is a "significant speedup". — [erosion.rs](https://docs.veloren.net/src/veloren_world/sim/erosion.rs.html)
- Beyer's droplet CPU cost is ~0.3–0.4 ms per drop at radius 4 (100k drops ≈ 32–44 s). Lague's repo mentions a compute-shader variant in its video, but the fetched README contains no timings. — [Beyer thesis](https://ardordeosis.github.io/implementation-of-a-method-for-hydraulic-erosion/thesis-beyer.pdf); [SebLague/Hydraulic-Erosion](https://github.com/SebLague/Hydraulic-Erosion)
- Schott 2023 claims interactive stream-power authoring through a fast drainage-area approximation (details not retrievable here). — [SIGGRAPH history entry](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=132308)

### Inferences
- For the stated goal (terrain that looks water-carved, plus rivers) the deciding cost is the coarse global drainage, which is CPU-friendly and one-off per seed; GPU acceleration does not change the architecture. A GPU pipe-model pass would be most useful as a *detail* stage on a chunk-plus-halo tile (Šťava-style border copying) to add gullies and alluvial fans at block resolution; because that stage runs in lockstep over a tile it must be deterministic per tile seed and re-run identically on both sides of a chunk border, or the result must be computed once per region and cached.
- Droplet erosion on the GPU (one thread per droplet writing with atomics) is the cheapest route to "looks eroded" at block resolution, but droplets interfere through the heightmap, so results are order-dependent and non-deterministic unless the implementation serialises writes — a real problem for a pure per-chunk function.

### Gaps
- No Vulkan-specific erosion implementation with published timings was found; all GPU figures are OpenGL/DirectX-era.
- Schott 2023's GPU/CPU split and per-iteration time remain unknown.

## Key question 6: Thermal erosion and talus for cliffs and scree; combining with ridged noise

### Takeaway
Thermal erosion is a purely local stencil (compare slope to a talus angle, move the excess downhill), so it is the one erosion process that can run per chunk with a one-cell halo; its parameters are an angle (published values: 6°–54° height-dependent in Cordonnier, 15°–60° from rock-strength noise in Veloren, tangent 0.8×+0.1 in Jákó, 0.05 talus/0.25 transfer in a Rust crate) and an iteration count, and it is what turns ridged-noise spikes into cliffs with scree aprons.

### Cited Findings

- Musgrave, Kolb, Mace 1989 introduced thermal (and hydraulic) erosion on a 2D cell array where material moves based on the gradient to each neighbour, with the talus angle as the threshold below which nothing moves (per Benes 2001's description). — [Benes 2001 (search summary)](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Benes-2001-Parallel_Implementation_of_Terrain_Erosion_Applied_to_the_Surfac.pdf)
- Basic rule (Marák, CESCG 1997): where the slope between a vertex and a neighbour exceeds talus angle T, material moves from the higher to the lower point, amount proportional to the slope. — [search summary, CESCG97](https://old.cescg.org/CESCG97/marak/node11.html)
- Jákó & Tóth: eight virtual pipes for thermal erosion; local talus tan α = (b − b_i)/d; the set of neighbours under the talus angle receives soil proportionally; parameters K_t = 0.15 (thermal rate), K_a = 0.8 and K_i = 0.1 (talus tangent coefficient and bias). — [Jákó & Tóth 2011](https://www.old.cescg.org/CESCG-2011/papers/TUBudapest-Jako-Balazs.pdf)
- Šťava: slippage moves Δt (Δh − l tan α) when Δh > l tan α, per material layer with its own talus angle; layers processed bottom-to-top. — [Šťava et al. 2008](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Stava08SCA.pdf)
- Cordonnier: a height-dependent maximal slope for thermal erosion gives "the succession of cliffs and slopes"; min/max slope angles 6° and 54°; "we can obtain interesting features by procedurally adjusting the thermal slope". — [Cordonnier et al. 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Cordonier16CGF.pdf)
- Veloren: talus limit from a 3D rock-strength noise field mapped (logit/logistic centred at 0.4, scaled by mountain_scale) to angles between 15° and 60°, mid-slope 30°; above the limit material collapses to the lowest neighbour, below it a nonlinear hillslope transport term applies, capped at min_length²/dt for stability; separate diffusivities for sediment (kdsed) and bedrock (kd). — [erosion.rs](https://docs.veloren.net/src/veloren_world/sim/erosion.rs.html)
- A Rust crate (symbios-ground) exposes: iteration count, talus angle default 0.05, transfer fraction default 0.25 (clamped at 0.25 for stability), a water level, and a separate underwater talus angle default 0.1; "smaller values produce gentler, more smoothed terrain; larger values leave steeper cliffs intact". — [symbios-ground thermal.rs](https://docs.rs/crate/symbios-ground/0.3.0/source/src/erosion/thermal.rs)
- Unity Terrain Tools describes the talus value as a resting angle: "slopes that are steeper than this angle will erode, while slopes less steep will remain untouched". — [Unity Terrain Tools, Thermal Erosion](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.terrain-tools@5.3/manual/erosion-thermal.html)
- Ridged noise: r(p) = 1 − |n(p)| gives sharp crests; Red Blob weights higher-octave ridges by the lower-octave result so detail appears mainly on mountains, and suggests mixing ridged low-frequency with non-ridged high-frequency noise. — [Red Blob Games, terrain from noise](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/terrain-from-noise/); [Galin et al. review (search summary)](https://wenku.csdn.net/doc/3h4apwpp1n)
- Jákó & Tóth: hydraulic erosion alone "carves deep grooves … that rarely occurs in nature", which is why thermal erosion is combined with it. — [Jákó & Tóth 2011](https://www.old.cescg.org/CESCG-2011/papers/TUBudapest-Jako-Balazs.pdf)

### Inferences
- Thermal erosion with the transfer fraction clamped at 0.25 and a handful of iterations is cheap enough to run on a chunk-plus-halo column heightmap at generation time (a few passes over 34×34 cells), and since it only looks at immediate neighbours, a halo of (iterations) cells makes it deterministic across borders.
- Using a noise-driven talus angle (Veloren's 15°–60° from rock strength) is the simplest way to get both cliff bands and scree slopes from the same pass; Cordonnier's height-dependent slope limit gives stacked cliffs.
- Ridged noise supplies the sharp ridge lines that erosion alone takes many iterations to produce; thermal erosion then caps their slopes, and the carved channels from the drainage map provide the valleys — the three together are the standard "looks eroded without full simulation" recipe.

### Gaps
- Musgrave's original 1989 parameter values were not retrieved (secondary sources only).

## Key question 7: Published parameter values and runtimes (consolidated)

### Takeaway
Representative figures: droplets 70k–300k per 512²–1024² map at ~0.3–0.4 ms each (Beyer: 100k drops ≈ 32–44 s CPU; McDonald: 200k particles ≈ 10–20 s); GPU pipe model 1024² at 15–160 ms/iteration (Jákó) and 2048×1024 at ≥ 20 fps (Šťava); stream power 160k nodes at 1.27 s/iteration with ~200 iterations to converge (Cordonnier), 1024² grid at ~1.25 s/iteration (Lim 2022 reimplementation); plate-local rivers ~3–4 ms per ~6.7k-unit region and 1.1 µs per sample (Davis); Genevaux network+terrain in ~10 s for hundreds of km².

- Beyer Table 6.1/6.2 (CPU): 5k/10k/20k/50k/100k drops on 1024² = 2.29/2.56/8.60/20.90/40.65 s; radius 1→8 on 10k drops/256² = 0.48→9.69 s; defaults p_inertia 0.3, p_capacity 8, p_deposition 0.2, p_erosion 0.7, p_evaporation 0.02, p_minSlope 0.01, p_gravity 10, p_radius 4, p_maxPath 64. — [Beyer thesis](https://ardordeosis.github.io/implementation-of-a-method-for-hydraulic-erosion/thesis-beyer.pdf)

### Inferences
- Scaling Cordonnier's CPU numbers (~8 µs per node per iteration at 160k nodes) to a 1024² coarse map gives ~8 s/iteration single-threaded, ~1–2 s with 8 threads; 50 iterations therefore lands around 1–2 minutes per seed, consistent with Veloren's observed startup time.
